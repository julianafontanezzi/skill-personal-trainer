# In-person service and gym management

Use when the request is about an instructor's work on the gym floor, client retention or hiring.

## The four pillars of the instructor

1. **Behavioral profile:** communication, energy and connection with the client. When hiring, prioritize behavior and character; it is easier to teach technique to a communicative person than to teach communication to someone closed off. A practical test of 2 to 3 hours on the floor, after the interviews, is worthwhile.
2. **360° vision:** stand in the corners of the room to see everything, actively look for who needs help and go to the client before being called. Like the attentive waiter: clients only ask for help if they feel someone is looking.
3. **Empathy and good judgment:** know the goal of each client on your roster and adapt training, language and level of demand. A workout that is too hard drives away people seeking health and leisure; one that is too light frustrates people seeking performance. Ask a lot and request feedback.
4. **Communication:** chin level (neither arrogant nor submissive), open posture, balanced gestures; speech with no swearing, with clear volume, pace and diction. Often how you say it matters more than what you say. It is worth studying public speaking and body language.

## Professional conduct

- A lifting professional must master exercise physiology, anatomy and biomechanics.
- Do not criticize another client's technique from a distance: it may be the best that person can do (e.g., a woman with an elbow prosthesis who cannot fully extend her arm). Only gross errors are clearly wrong.
- A floor instructor with many clients can gather small groups to explain RM and the two rules.
- Lifting does not shorten muscles: with correct technique and full range of motion it improves flexibility. Children can lift; older adults gain bone, balance and coordination.
- Sharing knowledge is part of the job of a fitness professional.

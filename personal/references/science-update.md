# Science update

The core of this skill comes from a course recorded around 2021. The **practical logic** of the course (tension vs metabolic, ranges instead of fixed numbers, the two max-effort rules, order from tension to metabolic, 6 to 12 weeks per phase) is still useful. Some **physiological explanations** and some numbers are simplified or outdated compared with recent strength-training literature. Use the "how to explain today" column when talking to the client.

The points below reflect the general consensus of the field (reviews and meta-analyses in the strength-training literature). When a client asks for the exact source of a number, say it is consensus from reviews and avoid inventing references.

| Topic | The course says | How to explain today | Effect on programming |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lactic acid | lactic acid buildup "denatures" the fiber (like lemon on meat) and causes micro-tears; the burn is lactic acid | lactate is not the villain: it is cleared in about an hour and **does not cause next-day soreness**. The burn comes from the buildup of several metabolites. Metabolic stress **contributes** to hypertrophy, but with weaker evidence than mechanical tension | keep metabolic techniques as a complement, not the base |
| Main driver of hypertrophy | three stimuli of similar weight (calcium, tension, lactic acid) | the main driver is **mechanical tension** with high fiber recruitment, which happens when the set gets **close to failure**, at any reasonable load | prioritize effective sets (RIR 0 to 3) and load progression |
| Micro-tears and soreness | micro-tears and inflammation are a necessary part of the process | muscle damage is **not required** for growth; too much damage hurts recovery. **Next-day soreness is not a measure of workout quality** | do not chase soreness; volume that leaves a person sore for many days should be reduced |
| Rep range | 8 to 12 is the hypertrophy range; 13 to 20 low hypertrophy | hypertrophy is **similar from roughly 6 to 30 reps** if the set gets close to failure. Heavy loads favor strength; very light loads (under ~30% of 1RM) are less reliable | 8 to 12 remains the most practical range (efficient, low joint stress, less discomfort); use 6 to 8 and 15 to 25 as variations, or for people with pain at heavy loads |
| Muscular failure | Rule 1: go to the max in every set | sets at **0 to 3 RIR** produce similar hypertrophy; failure on every set raises fatigue and risk on free weights | use the agreed RIR; more failure on isolations and machines, rarely on heavy compounds |
| Rest | multi-joint 1 to 2 min; isolations 30 to 60 s | short rest drops reps in later sets. On **heavy compounds, 2 to 3 min** tends to give slightly better results; isolations at 60 to 90 s work well | compounds: 2 to 3 min (down to 1 to 2 min if time is short); isolations: 60 to 90 s; short rest as an occasional metabolic strategy |
| Volume | per session: 12 to 15 sets (1x/week) or 8 to 10 (2x/week) | weekly volume of **~10 to 20 sets per muscle** is well supported for trained people; beginners grow with less. With equal weekly volume, frequency matters little, but 2x/week makes distribution easier | the course's numbers (16 to 20 weekly sets at 2x) fit this range; start with the lowest volume that produces progress |
| Speed | tension work should be slow; emphasis on the eccentric | reps of ~0.5 to 6 s produce similar hypertrophy; **super slow (over 10 s) is worse**. Controlled lowering is good for technique and safety | controlled lowering (2 to 3 s), with no need for extreme slowness |
| Hormones | insulin, GH and testosterone from training and carbohydrate explain hypertrophy | post-workout hormone spikes within the normal range **explain little** of muscle gain. What matters is well-done training, enough protein and calories, and sleep | talk about sleep and food as recovery, without promising "hormone spikes" |
| Vascular occlusion | the tourniquet alone gives a small hypertrophy; do not do it at home | **blood flow restriction (BFR) training** exists, at 20 to 30% of 1RM, and produces real hypertrophy. It needs proper cuffs and supervision | do not prescribe BFR without your own training and equipment |
| 1RM test | only used in research | estimating from the reps x RIR relationship is practical and safe; 1RM testing has a place for strength athletes | estimate loads from reps; 1RM only for those training strength with good technique |
| ATP-CP recovery | 30 s ≈ 60%, 3 to 5 min ≈ 100% | right order of magnitude (~50% in 30 s, nearly full in 3 to 5 min); these are approximations | can be used to explain rest |
| GVT | the source cites 30 s rest | the original version uses **60 to 90 s** | use 60 to 90 s |
| Stretching beforehand | no hard stretching before | long static stretching (over ~60 s per muscle) right before can slightly reduce strength; short or dynamic does not interfere | dynamic warm-up + ramp-up sets |

## What still holds from the course, without caveats

- Prescribe in **ranges** and adjust load set by set; lifting is "biological, not mathematical".
- **Rule 2:** the maximum must fall inside the range; adjust the load to make it so.
- Start the session with the most tension-oriented and compound work, finish with metabolic and isolations.
- Change the stimulus every 6 to 12 weeks.
- Beginners and people returning from a break respond to little stimulus.
- Recovery (sleep, food, 48 to 72 h between heavy sessions for the same muscle) is part of training.
- Lifting is safe and useful for children, older adults and bone health, with proper guidance.
